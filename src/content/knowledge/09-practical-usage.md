## How to apply the tie to a trailer or container door

1. Close both trailer doors and set the vertical locking bars into the top and
   bottom keepers.
2. Loop the tie tail through the door handle keeper and around the vertical
   locking bar or the second door handle, depending on the trailer hardware
   pattern.
3. Feed the tail into the head with the pawl side facing inward, and pull the
   tail through until the loop is snug but not overtightened.
4. Give the tail a firm tug to confirm the pawl has engaged and the head cannot
   be pulled back through.
5. Read and record the color and (if serialized) the printed number before the
   driver leaves the dock.

Snug means the tie should not slide loosely around the handle; over-tight means
bending the door hardware or stressing the tie head. A properly seated tie moves
freely around the handle circumference but cannot be pulled off without cutting.

## How to remove the tie

The tie is single-use and must be cut to remove. Use a pair of side-cutters,
diagonal pliers, or a seal-cutter tool. Do not attempt to release the pawl by
prying. The head is designed to not release, and prying will only deform the
head and leave inconclusive tamper evidence.

Cut through the tie body between the head and the door hardware, then dispose of
both halves. Cut ties should not be reused, re-tied around a smaller loop, or
handed to another driver.

## Reusability

The tie is strictly single-use. Once the pawl is seated, the tie cannot be
reopened without damage. This is a feature, not a limitation: single-use
construction is what makes the tie tamper-evident. Reusable seals are not
tamper-evident by definition.

## Installation temperature

The recommended installation temperature range is 0 °C to +85 °C (32 °F to
185 °F). Nylon 66 remains functional well below the recommended installation
temperature. The tie will still install and hold at -40 °F, but the material
becomes stiffer as it gets colder and the pawl requires more force to fully
seat. In sub-freezing conditions, take an extra second to confirm the pawl click
and give the tail a firmer tug than you would in summer.

## Cold weather use

Working temperature range extends to -40 °F. The tie will function through
northern-tier winter freight lanes, reefer service at low setpoints, and outdoor
yard storage in cold climates. Two operational notes:

- Ties become stiffer at low temperature. Confirm pawl seating with a firm tug.
- Ties applied at warm temperature and cooled to sub-zero will maintain their
  loop; ties applied at sub-zero temperature to warm hardware will hold once the
  hardware cools to match.

## Hot weather and full-sun exposure

Working temperature range extends to +185 °F. Trailer skin temperature under
full southern-summer sun can reach 160 °F without an issue for the tie.
Short-term excursions to +230 °F (+110 °C) are within the maximum sustained
rating. Do not use for continuous service inside a heated environment above
+185 °F.

## Chemical resistance

Good resistance to bases, oils, greases, and oil derivatives, which covers the
typical dock environment (diesel, hydraulic fluid, brake dust, road salt).
Limited resistance to chloride solvents; not resistant to phenols. For
fuel-splash environments such as a tanker discharge port, the tie is acceptable
for external door sealing, but do not install where the tie will sit in a
solvent pool.

## Storage and shelf life

Store unopened polybags in a cool, dry, indoor environment out of direct
sunlight. Under those conditions, the tie has a useful shelf life of
approximately 5 years from the manufacturing date printed on the lot code.

Ties stored in a hot outdoor shed for a season are still usable but should be
inspected for brittleness before deployment on a critical route. If a tie snaps
during a routine tug test, discard the affected polybag and open a fresh one.

## Batch and lot tracking

Every polybag has a printed lot code that ties back to the manufacturing run,
the material lot, and the ship date. If a lot needs to be traced, for a
warranty claim, an audit, or a customer-specific requirement, the lot code is
the identifier to provide to support. Shaw Safety maintains a rolling archive of
lot records for the useful shelf life of the product.

## Inspection at the receiving dock (VVTT)

The VVTT method (View, Verify, Tug, Twist) is standard for high-security ISO
17712 seal inspection but adapts cleanly to indicative ties as well.

- **View**: confirm the tie is present, the color matches the expected color
  for the lane or shift, and the head is not visibly damaged.
- **Verify**: read and record the printed number or color code and match it to
  the shipping paperwork.
- **Tug**: apply a firm pull to confirm the tie is seated and the loop has not
  been re-tied.
- **Twist**: twist the head and body slightly; a legitimate seated tie will not
  release, and a tie that has been cut and re-tied will show at this step.

## Signs of tampering

- Head deformed, cracked, or discolored around the pawl.
- Body cut and re-tied with a shorter loop.
- Color mismatch against the expected lane or shift color.
- Serial number (if used) mismatched against the shipping paperwork.
- Head that releases or shifts under a light tug.

Any of these signals should trigger the receiver's tampered-seal protocol. The
tie itself is not a barrier. It is an indicator. The purpose of the indication
is to prompt a shipment integrity check, not to have already prevented entry.

## Bundle diameter: matching the tie to the hardware

Maximum bundle diameter is 76.2 mm (approximately 3 inches). This covers the
door handle keeper geometry of standard intermodal containers, 53-foot domestic
containers, dry vans, and refrigerated trailers. Minimum bundle diameter is
3 mm, which is well below any trailer hardware.

If the hardware requires a loop larger than 3 inches, for example wrapping
around an oversized handle stub or a doubled-up locking bar, the 11-inch tie is
not the right size and a longer tie is needed. Contact sales before assuming it
will reach.

## Cutting tools and driver kits

A small pair of side-cutters is the most common tool. Some fleets standardize on
a seal-cutter tool with a built-in tail guide, which is faster in the cold and
reduces the risk of nicking a knuckle. Every driver should carry a cutter. A tie
the driver cannot cut at the next stop delays the delivery.

## Serial number recording

For fleets using custom-printed serial numbers, record the serial at both
application and removal. The recording can be as simple as a driver photo of the
seated tie sent to dispatch, or as involved as a barcode scan into a TMS. Serial
recording is what turns an indicative tie into a chain-of-custody artifact.
Without a recorded number, an intact tie only proves that some tie was on the
door.
